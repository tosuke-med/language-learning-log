## Today's topic
Email writing

## Today's practice phrases
I regret to inform you that your article would not be acceptable for this journal.
I apologize for the late response.
Please accept my apologize for the cancellation.
Please find the attached file.
Allow me to taking your offer.
Would it be possible to send me a sample you receive last year?

## Correction and explanation from LLM

Here are corrections and explanations for each phrase:

**1. "I regret to inform you that your article would not be acceptable for this journal."**
→ *I regret to inform you that your article **will not be** acceptable for this journal.*
(or: *…is not acceptable for this journal.*)

"Would not be" implies a hypothetical or conditional situation, but here you are stating a definite editorial decision. Use "will not be" for a firm future statement, or "is not" if the decision has already been made. "I regret to inform you that…" is a classic softening phrase (cushion expression) used to deliver bad news politely — very common in formal English correspondence.

---

**2. "I apologize for the late response."**
→ No correction needed. ✓

This is a standard and natural apology phrase. "I apologize for + noun/gerund" is correct. Alternatively, "I'm sorry for the delayed response" or "Sorry for the late reply" work in less formal contexts.

---

**3. "Please accept my apologize for the cancellation."**
→ *Please accept my **apologies** for the cancellation.*

"Apologize" is a verb; the noun form needed here is "apology" (singular) or "apologies" (plural). "Please accept my apologies" is the set phrase — using the plural sounds more formal and sincere. "Please accept my…" is another cushion expression that softens a negative message.

---

**4. "Please find the attached file."**
→ No correction needed. ✓

This is one of the most common phrases in professional email writing. "Please find attached…" or "Please find the attached file" are both standard. You may also see "I have attached…" in slightly more casual emails.

---

**5. "Allow me to taking your offer."**
→ *Allow me to **take** your offer.*

After "allow me to," use the base form of the verb (infinitive without "to" is already expressed by "to" before the verb). "Taking" (gerund/present participle) is incorrect here. "Allow me to…" is a formal and polite phrase for accepting something or asking permission — another useful cushion expression.

---

**6. "Would it be possible to send me a sample you receive last year?"**
→ *Would it be possible to send me a sample you **received** last year?*

"Last year" refers to a past time, so the verb must be in the past tense: "received." "Would it be possible to…?" is a very useful polite request form using the modal "would" — it sounds more tentative and considerate than "Can you…?" or "Please send me…". Mastering "would" and "could" in requests is key to writing natural, polite English emails.
